02 · The Frameworks

Two faces of legality

The Company's story hinges on the gap between these two standards. Hover each pillar for its meaning — then watch the case files and ledger test every episode against both.

Lawful on paper

Formal Domestic Legality

Everything the Company did carried a charter, a statute, a treaty or a receipt — valid under the law of the day. This is rule by law at its most refined.

  • I

    Chartered authority

    Royal charters and Acts of Parliament conferred monopoly, coinage, armies and territory as lawful delegations of sovereign power.

  • II

    Statutory form

    Treaties, prize rules and revenue regulations put every extraction into written, sealed, enforceable legal form.

  • III

    Contractual precision

    Bills of sale, itemized ledgers and signed distribution lists — meticulous paperwork for every transfer.

  • IV

    Jurisdictional cover

    Company courts and the home legal system together legitimized corporate sovereignty abroad.

The test: is there a document? — almost always, yes.
Just in substance

Substantive Rule of Law / Ethics

Law is only law in the full sense when it rests on consent, treats everyone as equal, and protects rather than extracts. By this standard, the paperwork proves nothing.

  • I

    Consent of the governed

    Legitimacy requires the governed to be able to shape and question the law — not be ruled by a distant board.

  • II

    Equality before the law

    Those the law taxed and dispossessed had no standing in the courts that ruled them.

  • III

    Protection, not extraction

    Law must guard life, liberty and culture — not drain them into a shareholder's ledger.

  • IV

    No plunder, no duress

    A taking signed by a child sovereign at gunpoint, or seized from a dead ruler, is not justice however tidy the filing.

The test: would it be lawful if done to us? — almost always, no.

The Ultimate Legal Shield: The British Museum Act of 1963

A modern statute that keeps colonial-era acquisitions frozen in place

Section 5 legally forbids the Museum's Board of Trustees from disposing of — "deaccessioning" — any object in its permanent collections. The only permitted exceptions are narrow: physical duplicates, printed matter made after 1850, or objects "unfit to be retained" (effectively ruined). For everything else, removal is not merely discouraged — it is legally prohibited.

The legal paradox: a 1963 statute of a sovereign parliament is used to freeze the spoils of an earlier empire. The Rosetta Stone and the Benin Bronzes sit in the British Museum; the Koh-i-Noor sits with the Crown Jewels at the Tower of London. All of them now lie beyond the reach of restitution — under present domestic law, returning them is literally illegal.

Formal legality, deployed as a permanent lock on looted heritage.
